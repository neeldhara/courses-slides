§ 2

Similarity, made geometric

Treat each row as a point. Treat similarity as how close two points are.
Simple idea, surprisingly powerful.

The pivotal move

So far our data lives in a spreadsheet. Rows are users, columns are features.

Let's change one thing: imagine each row not as a row, but as a point in space, with one coordinate per feature.

Similar people → nearby points.

Now "find similar users" becomes a geometry question. We have tools for those.

does this move always capture what we mean by "similar"? When might it lie?

Three flavours of similarity

Euclidean distance — "How far apart are these two points?"

\(d(a,b) = \sqrt{\sum_i (a_i - b_i)^2}\)

Best for: absolute values matter on their own scale — fraud detection, GPS, sensor anomalies.

Cosine similarity — "Do they point the same way?"

\(\cos\theta = \dfrac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|}\)

Best for: proportions matter, magnitudes are noise — documents of different lengths, taste profiles.

Dot product — "Aligned and strong?"

\(a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta\)

Best for: recommendation scores where both alignment and magnitude carry information.

For today, we'll stay with Euclidean — it's the most concrete — but keep in mind the choice always matters.

§ 3

Linear transforms, geometrically

A short detour. To make sense of "good projection" later,
we need to see what a matrix does to a plane.

A matrix as a transformation of space

You can read the 2×2 matrix   [[a, b], [c, d]]  as instructions:

  • Send the standard right-arrow (1, 0) to (a, c).
  • Send the standard up-arrow (0, 1) to (b, d).

Why is that enough?

Every vector \((x,y) = x\,e_1 + y\,e_2\).

If \(f\) is linear, then \(f(x,y) = x\,f(e_1) + y\,f(e_2)\).

So once you know where the two basis arrows go, you know where every vector goes.

what should the matrix look like to rotate the plane 90°? To stretch it without losing the plane?

Matrix multiplication, as columns

The two columns are exactly where the basis vectors land.

In coordinates, the same story is:

\( \begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\ y\end{bmatrix} \)

\(=\; x\,\)\(\begin{bmatrix}a\\ c\end{bmatrix}\) (image of \(e_1\))

\(\phantom{=}\;+\; y\,\)\(\begin{bmatrix}b\\ d\end{bmatrix}\) (image of \(e_2\))

\(=\; \begin{bmatrix}ax + by\\ cx + dy\end{bmatrix}\).

Where the basis goes, the world follows

Demo: every linear map takes the circle to an ellipse

Same controls. Now the unit circle (gray) is being morphed by the matrix. The orange axes are the principal axes of the resulting ellipse — they're the longest and shortest diameters.

Singular values, seen with your eyes

What the ellipse is telling us

Every linear map sends the unit circle to an ellipse (possibly degenerate — a line, or even a point).

The longest axis of that ellipse is the direction in which the map stretches the most. The shortest axis is the direction it stretches the least.

The lengths of those two axes have a name: singular values.

The directions on the input side that produce them have a name: right singular vectors.

You've just met SVD, geometrically. The whole machinery is bookkeeping for: which direction does this matrix stretch the most, second-most, third-most, … in higher dimensions.

§ 4

Projections, good and bad

Now we need to collapse dimensions.
Not all collapses are equally lossy.

The coin, revisited

Three pictures of the same object:

  • A circle (face-on)
  • A flat oval (45°)
  • A thin rectangle (edge-on)

All three are projections of a coin from 3D onto a 2D screen. Same object — vastly different amounts of information.

The whole problem of dimensionality reduction:

find the projection that loses the least information about the structure you care about.

Drag the vector (its tip) and the projection line (its handle on the unit circle). The line is fixed through the origin; the projection is the shadow the vector casts on it.

Some angles are honest; some lie

What makes a projection "good"?

Looking at the two-cluster preset: the good line is the one that spreads the projected points out the most.

The big idea (the heart of PCA / SVD):

The "best" 1D projection of a 2D dataset is the line along which the projected points have the largest variance.

That direction has a name: the first principal axis. It's exactly the longest axis of the ellipse we saw in §3.

In higher dimensions, the same idea iterates:

find the direction of maximum variance, project, look at what's left, find the next direction of maximum variance, and so on.

That's the entire recipe behind principal component analysis, which the next lecture will derive from SVD.

§ 5

The miracle, and what's next

Johnson–Lindenstrauss tells us low-dimensional views exist.
SVD tells us how to find a good one.

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss theorem

Claim. Given any \(n\) points in any high-dimensional space, you can project them down to \(k \approx \dfrac{8 \ln n}{\varepsilon^2}\) dimensions such that every pairwise distance is preserved within a factor of \(1 \pm \varepsilon\).

The number of target dimensions doesn't depend on how many dimensions you started with.

In plain English:

If all you care about is which points are close to which other points, you can almost always squash to ~50–100 dimensions, regardless of where you started.

Every time you double the number of points, you only need to add a constant number of new dimensions.

stop and feel how strange this is. The dimensionality of the source data is irrelevant. The only thing that matters is the count of points.

What random projections give us

If we project our data onto a random low-dimensional subspace, pairwise distances are preserved (approximately, with high probability).

That means downstream tasks — clustering, nearest-neighbour, recommendation — can run in the small space and get nearly the same answers as in the full one.

But random projections are random. We pay for that wastefulness with a few extra dimensions.

What if, instead of picking \(k\) directions at random, we picked the \(k\) best directions for the data we actually have?

Tomorrow: the best projection

We saw it twice already today:

  • In §3, every linear map sent the unit circle to an ellipse with two characteristic axes.
  • In §4, the "good" projection of a 2D dataset was the line along which the projected points had the largest variance.

Singular Value Decomposition is the operation that finds those axes for any data matrix, in any dimension.

\(A = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top\)

\(U\) holds the output directions, \(V\) holds the input directions, \(\Sigma\) holds the stretch factors — sorted from largest to smallest, so the most important direction comes first.

Truncating after the top \(k\) singular values gives you the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation of your data. That's the whole game.

What to take with you today

1   Rows of data ≡ points in space

Once you've made that move, similarity becomes a geometry question. Every algorithm we'll meet builds on this.

2   The curse is real

In high dimensions, distances stop discriminating. Doing nothing is not an option.

3   Matrices act on space

They take circles to ellipses. The ellipse's axes are the singular values. Hold this picture.

4   Some projections lose almost nothing

JL: even random low-dim projections preserve distances. SVD: there's a uniquely best one.

5   SVD is the punchline

"Find the best directions, ranked by importance, throw away the rest." That's the next lecture.
